Forecasting rider fare pressure

A controlled comparison of time-series foundation forecasting and strong statistical and machine-learning baselines, with economic simulation kept distinct from empirical evidence.

Victor H. Aguiar · Interview discussion artifact · 26 October 2026 23:43 UTC

What the held-out data show

The validation-selected Chronos-2 + calendar/count has 18.6% lower MAE than Four-week seasonal on held-out NYC fares.

The unadapted, covariate-informed model also has 9.4% lower observed MAE than tuned gradient boosting ($1.07 versus $1.18). This is a descriptive test comparison. Its high-fare MAE is $2.55, versus $2.48 for Four-week seasonal; nominal 80% intervals cover 76.6% of outcomes. Synthetic adaptation adds no material improvement in this bounded pilot; validation retains the original model.

A horizon of h hours predicts the pickup-hour bin ending at origin + h, not an instantaneous price. Equal weight per observed route–origin–horizon case. Forecasts share histories and market conditions and are not independent trials. A short NYC panel does not establish universal superiority.

The outcome is the hourly median realized rider fare before tips, tolls, taxes and fees. It is neither an upfront price quote nor an identified surge premium.

A fixed example, not a selected success

What makes this comparison credible

Information available at the decision time

  • Route selection uses January only. Hyperparameter and model-variant selection use chronological validation. Selected learned baselines are refitted once on training plus available validation outcomes before testing.
  • The completed-trip history is embargoed until each pickup hour ends plus three hours. All methods forecast the same target bins.
  • Historical fares and served-trip activity are permitted. Future calendar features are known in advance. Future fares, realized trip duration and future trip counts are not predictors.
  • Missing target hours remain missing. UTC hourly grids handle daylight saving; rider behavior calendar features use New York local time.
  • No live quotes, private Uber data, driver compensation, or Uber One membership data are used.

Model and sample controls

  • The original Chronos-2 variants use frozen weights hash-identical to the October 30, 2025 release, preceding the 2026 outcomes. Any simulation-adapted variant is identified separately.
  • All forecasts run locally. No fine-tuning on the test window and no cross-learning across different forecast origins.
  • Statistical seasonality baselines, regularized lagged regression, and histogram gradient boosting provide substantive comparisons.
  • TLC publishes monthly files with a delay. This is an offline pseudo-online experiment, not a deployable public real-time feed.
  • Trip mix can change within a route, including unobserved service tiers. Fare predictability is not evidence of a causal pricing effect.

Economic simulation and synthetic-only adaptation

The economic prior links common rider prices and quoted pickup ETA to heterogeneous riders’ willingness to request a trip. It is motivated by the price–time choice problem in Buchholz, Doval, Kastl, Matějka and Salz (Econometrica, 2025), not a replication or use of their estimated parameters.

What the simulator assumes

  • Two-route, hourly capacity/queue episodes with positive, heterogeneous price and time sensitivities and an outside option.
  • FIFO service and explicit cancellations conserve the request queue. Pending requests retain their quoted fares.
  • Driver capacity is exogenous across pricing rules. Rider prices do not automatically increase driver pay or supply.
  • Parameters, shocks, scarcity and policy rules vary across episodes. NYC training data supply only coarse fare/count scales, not estimated elasticities.
  • This is not a fleet-conserving spatial equilibrium, an estimated demand system, or a production matching engine.

Adaptation contract

  • 96 simulated episodes × 2 routes × 1008 hours. Low-count fare outcomes remain missing.
  • 60 local LoRA steps; rank 8, learning rate 0.00001, context 256 hours, nine future steps. Five calendar covariates are known ahead; served count is historical only.
  • 1,206,912 trainable parameters. Measured optimization time: 45.3 seconds.
  • No real validation/test outcomes enter gradient updates. Step 0 remains a legitimate selection option.

Model-dependent policy scenarios

Compare rules only within the same world, where potential arrivals, capacity and choice uniforms are held fixed. Worlds differ in multiple parameters. The anticipatory rule uses calendar information and lagged potential arrivals; it does not use Chronos forecasts and requires arrival information absent from TLC. Quoted ETA is a proxy, not realized waiting time. Gross rider fares exclude driver costs and are not profit. These are synthetic outcomes, not treatment effects at Uber.

How I would take this into a science–engineering partnership

From forecast to a usable system

  1. Agree on the decision, the data contract and an explicit availability/latency budget with engineering.
  2. Validate quotes and rider-fare decomposition. Monitor freshness, coverage, calibration and errors during high-pressure periods.
  3. Run a shadow forecast with a robust seasonal fallback. Jointly own interfaces, tests, rollout gates and monitoring.
  4. Use controlled evaluation to decide whether the forecast improves the actual product decision, not only offline error.

Economic questions that need internal evidence

  • Does anticipating high fares improve conversion, waiting choices, reliability or longer-run retention?
  • When does partial surge protection for subscribers create incremental value rather than subsidize rides that would happen anyway?
  • How do competitor prices, availability and alternative products change rider substitution?
  • Evaluate contribution margin and marketplace reliability alongside rider outcomes. Public completed-trip data alone cannot answer these questions.
